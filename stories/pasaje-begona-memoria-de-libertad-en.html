Pasaje Begoña, a memory of freedom

From encounters and diversity in the 1960s to the great raid of 1971: why this passage matters far beyond Torremolinos

From encounters and diversity in the 1960s to the great raid of 1971: why this passage matters far beyond Torremolinos.

Before the raid: a place of coexistence

In the final months of 1962, establishments frequented by LGBTQ+ people began opening in Pasaje Begoña, in a town attracting travellers, workers and artists of different nationalities. The town council's tourism portal explains that the passage became a landmark of coexistence and diversity, with a nightlife atmosphere unusual in Francoist Spain. [1]

This relative openness had limits. The freedom experienced inside some venues coexisted with a repressive political regime, restrictive social norms and police surveillance. Historical research by the Pasaje Begoña Association allows us to examine this contradiction without idealising it. [2]

The memory of places such as Toni's Bar, whose opening in 1962 is documented by the municipal blog, belongs to that early period. Pioneering spaces should be described carefully: expressions such as “the first” or “the only” depend on the historical documentation available and may require qualification. [3]

24 June 1971: the great raid

On the night of 24 June 1971, police conducted a major operation in Pasaje Begoña and nearby streets. The association's sources and municipal tourism sources agree on the seriousness of the episode and its impact on those who were identified or arrested, and on establishments that suffered fines or closures. [2] [4]

How many people were detained? Published figures are not entirely consistent. An educational resource from the association mentions 114 arrests and more than 300 identifications; its historical research also notes that most sources agree on more than a hundred detentions. To avoid false precision, this edition refers to more than one hundred people detained and records the variation between documents. [2] [4]

The episode was not merely a police intervention in an entertainment district. It exposed the extent to which people's emotional lives and expression of identity could be persecuted under the dictatorship. It affected specific people, specific establishments and the image of a town beginning to acquire international prominence.

There are places whose meaning is written not in their architecture but in what happened within their walls.

A recovered memory

Over time, associations and researchers have worked to preserve the memory of the passage. The Pasaje Begoña Association has promoted research and educational projects, as well as commemorative activities related to the raid. In 2021 it organised initiatives connected with its fiftieth anniversary in several Spanish cities. [2] [5]

The municipal tourism site presents Pasaje Begoña as a place of historical memory and symbolically links it to Stonewall Inn in New York. The comparison helps explain its significance for LGBTQ+ history, but does not mean that the two events were identical or that they shared the same political circumstances. [1]

Rigorous public remembrance does not need exaggeration. It needs documents, context, spaces for listening and a clear commitment to conveying what happened without turning other people's suffering into a mere publicity device.

Why visit it today

Those walking through the centre of Torremolinos can stop at Pasaje Begoña as they would visit a small open-air archive. It is not a monument that explains itself: it takes on meaning when one understands what it represented in the 1960s and what happened in June 1971.

The tourism portal provides information on guided visits connected with the association; the availability and conditions of any particular visit should be checked directly with the organisers beforehand. [1]

It is worth arriving with a simple question: what would it have meant to people at the time to find a space here where they could be themselves? From there, history becomes more human, more complex and more urgent.
